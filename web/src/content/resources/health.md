This page exists because the rest of the site would be indefensible without it. A sport
judged on appearance, played by people aged 18 to 22, carries risks that a training article
will not mention.

## Start here: your campus

Every NCBO member is, by definition, attached to a university. That makes the two most useful
resources on this page ones you already have:

- **Campus counselling / student mental health services.** Usually free or low-cost, already
  paid for by your fees, and confidential.
- **Campus sports medicine / student health.** The people to see about fatigue, injuries that
  will not heal, a period that has stopped, or a diet you are not sure about.

They are more useful than any national number, because they can see you, and see you again
next week.

## Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs)

**REDs** is a syndrome of health and performance problems caused by **low energy
availability** — not eating enough to cover both training and the basic business of being
alive. It was defined by the International Olympic Committee, whose 2023 consensus statement
is the current reference.

Low energy availability is the **primary cause**. It sits on a spectrum: some short-term,
adaptable exposure is a normal part of training, while sustained or severe exposure becomes
problematic and produces the outcomes below. It affects **male and female athletes**.

This matters here specifically because contest prep *is* a deliberate energy deficit. A
bodybuilding prep is one of the few things a college student can do that produces prolonged
low energy availability on purpose.

### Health consequences

The consensus statement identifies effects across systems:

- **Reproductive dysfunction** — including menstrual disturbance and suppressed reproductive
  hormones in men
- **Impaired bone health**, including reduced bone mineral density and **stress injuries**
- **Metabolic and thyroid disruption**
- **Cardiovascular effects**
- **Gastrointestinal effects**
- **Immune suppression** — getting ill more often, and staying ill longer
- **Mental health deterioration**, including depression, anxiety and disordered eating

### Performance consequences

If the health list has not landed, this one usually does — the same deficit that causes the
above also costs you the thing you were dieting for:

- Reduced training response — the work stops turning into muscle
- Decreased strength and endurance
- More illness and more injuries, so less training done
- Impaired judgement and coordination

**Underfuelling does not make you better at this sport. It makes you worse at it, while
making you ill.** If your lifts have been falling for weeks, that is data, not weakness.

## Eating disorder risk in aesthetic sport

Sports judged on appearance carry elevated eating disorder risk. Bodybuilding normalises
behaviours — weighing food, tracking every gram, weighing yourself daily, eating on a
schedule regardless of hunger — that are also features of disordered eating. The behaviour
can look identical from outside. What differs is the relationship: whether you can stop, and
what happens when you do.

The Helms review explicitly recommends that competitors have **access to appropriate mental
health professionals**. That is not a footnote; a prep without that access is missing a
component.

Warning signs worth taking seriously in yourself or a training partner: eating rules that
keep tightening, distress when a meal is off-plan, avoiding eating with other people, unable
to take a rest day, weighing yourself many times a day, or a mood that now tracks the scale.

### Where to get help

- **National Alliance for Eating Disorders** — helpline **866-662-1235**, Monday to Friday,
  9am–7pm ET. Staffed by licensed clinicians; provides treatment referrals.
  [allianceforeatingdisorders.com](https://www.allianceforeatingdisorders.com/)
- **ANAD (National Association of Anorexia Nervosa and Associated Disorders)** — helpline
  **888-375-7767**, Monday to Friday, 9am–9pm CT. Free peer support, treatment referrals, and
  [material specifically for athletes](https://anad.org/get-informed/athletes-and-eating-disorders/).
  [anad.org](https://anad.org/)
- **Your campus counselling service** — first, and soonest.

> **A note on a number you will still see everywhere.** The NEDA helpline was discontinued in
> 2023 and no longer operates, but it continues to circulate in resource lists, printed
> handouts and old blog posts. If you are passing a number to someone in difficulty, check it
> is currently answering before you send it. A dead helpline at the wrong moment does real
> harm.

## If you take one thing from this page

Being lean for a show is temporary. The bone density you did not build at 20, the
relationship with food you learned in a prep, and the injuries you trained through are not.
Compete if you want to compete — a great many people do it without harm — but do it fed, and
with someone qualified watching.
